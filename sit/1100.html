What did the European broadband feeds just record?

Posted by Dave_Tremor_91 - 12 March 2012, 14:15 CET - 2 comments

I usually only post here to complain about the local freight line shaking my basement or to upload screen grabs of roadwork blasting out by the quarry.

I’ve been sitting in front of my monitors since 5:00 AM cross-referencing GEOFON feeds, open IRIS stations, and the trace from my own geophone (an old 4.5Hz GeoSpace element hooked into a 16-bit digitizer running AmaSeis on an old XP box). If anyone with an actual geophysics degree wants to look at the miniSEED files and tell me I botched my filter settings, please do. Because right now, nothing about this morning's telemetry adds up.

Here is the trace data from earlier today.

04:17 UTC - The first spike

At 04:17:02 UTC, my helicorder display jumped.

My initial assumption was a regional shaker somewhere under the Irish Sea or North Wales, maybe magnitude 3.8 to 4.2. But when I pulled the raw components and killed the low-pass filter, the waveform looked completely wrong.

A standard tectonic rupture gives you distinct P and S wave arrivals, crustal scatter, and rolling low-frequency coda. This was basically a clean, single-frequency pulse centered around 4.1 Hz. The energy stayed almost flat across the network. Stations out on the Atlantic margin picked it up at nearly the exact same ground velocity as inland broadband stations hundreds of kilometers away.

I jumped onto the Freenode #geology IRC channel to see if anyone else was seeing it. The two guys awake figured it was a bad NTP time-sync packet corrupting the seed buffers across a few European hubs, or maybe an unannounced naval depth-charge clearance.

Then 11:31 rolled around.

11:31 UTC - The inverted arrival

At 11:31:24 UTC, the feeds jumped again.

I pulled the SAC files from BGS, GEOFON, and a couple of open DIAS stations in Ireland to run a rough time-of-arrival calculation in WinQuake. With any normal event, the sensor closest to the hypocenter registers first, and the wavefront expands outward across the grid.

At 11:31, the arrival sequence ran backward.

The outer stations - coastal Scotland, western Brittany, the Spanish shelf - triggered first. The wave propagated inward toward the center. It collapsed inward across the Irish Sea basin and converged on a single point. When it converged, my local trace logged a brief, sharp mechanical pulse that died off almost immediately. No resonance ringing, no trailing coda.

The location (and my rough math)

I ran a standard trilateration using an IASP91 travel-time model, but because the apparent phase velocity across the regional array was ridiculously high (closer to 8.2 km/s than normal crustal P-wave speeds), the depth estimate is completely messy. WinQuake is spitting out a hypocenter down around 35 to 40 kilometers in the upper mantle, which makes no sense for something that registered with zero surface shear.

My surface coordinates put the epicenter somewhere out in the rural Midlands/Southeast border of Ireland, right in the scrub wetlands around County Wexford. Looking at Google Earth imagery, there's no active fault line, no heavy industry, and no military proving ground. It just centers near an old farm pond or drainage reservoir out past the tree line.

Then there’s the timing gap: 7 hours, 14 minutes, and 22 seconds between the first pulse and the return.

If this was a surface wave traveling along the crust at typical Rayleigh speeds (roughly 3.2 km/s), 26,062 seconds puts the travel distance at around 83,000 kilometers. That is slightly more than two complete trips around the equator, yet none of the global stations picked up the intermediate orbital passes you'd expect from a multi-orbit R1/R2 train.

If you assume it was an acoustic wave trapped in the oceanic SOFAR channel at 1.5 km/s, the distance works out to roughly 39,100 km - which is suspiciously close to the Earth's circumference - except the source point is sitting in muddy bedrock miles away from deep water.

My current guess is that we're looking at a bad telemetry loop on the regional hub servers, or maybe some bizarre British MoD low-frequency acoustic test in the Celtic Sea that bled into the coastal telemetry and fooled the software.

If anyone with institutional access can pull the raw unclipped logs from the DIAS network or the UK broadband arrays, let me know what you see. I put the raw .DAT export and my station list up on MediaFire; link is in the sidebar.
