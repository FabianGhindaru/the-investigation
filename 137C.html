The fine-structure constant, usually denoted α (alpha), is a dimensionless physical constant that quantifies the strength of the electromagnetic interaction between elementary charged particles. Because it is dimensionless, its numerical value is the same in every system of units — which is precisely what makes it interesting, and precisely what makes it strange.

Its value is approximately 0.007 297 352 5693, so its reciprocal is approximately 137.036. Physicists almost never quote α; they quote 1/α, and they round it to 137, and then they stare at it. The number is not derived from anything. It is measured, inserted into the theory by hand, and quietly left there.[1]

The constant appears wherever an electron meets a photon: in the spectrum of hydrogen, in the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron, in the Lamb shift, in the lifetime of positronium, in the opacity of stellar plasma. Its smallness — about 1/137 — is why light passes through a windowpane instead of being absorbed by the first layer of atoms, and it is why the universe is transparent.

Definition

In SI units the fine-structure constant is defined as

α = e2 / (4π ε0 ħ c) ≈ 1 / 137.036

where e is the elementary charge, ε0 the vacuum permittivity, ħ the reduced Planck constant and c the speed of light. In natural units, where ħ = c = 4πε0 = 1, the definition collapses to the pleasingly blunt statement α = e2.

Two further readings are common. First, α is the ratio of the velocity of an electron in the first circular orbit of the Bohr model to the speed of light; this is the historical origin of the name, since the ratio sets the size of the fine structure splittings in atomic spectra. Second, α is the squared coupling of quantum electrodynamics: the probability amplitude for an electron to emit or absorb a photon is proportional to √α, so every additional photon in a Feynman diagram costs a factor of about 1/137 in probability. This is why perturbation theory works, and why physicists who compute to five loops earn their keep.

The square root is itself a well-known quantity: √α ≈ 0.085 424 55. It is this number, not 137, that appears in the Feynman quote below — a detail almost everyone gets wrong when quoting it.

History

Sommerfeld and the splitting of lines

Arnold Sommerfeld introduced the constant in 1916 while extending the Bohr model to include elliptic orbits and relativistic corrections.[2] His calculation predicted that the hydrogen spectral lines should split into closely spaced components — a fine structure — with a spacing governed by the dimensionless ratio he called α. The prediction was confirmed, and the letter has stuck for one hundred and ten years.

Sommerfeld's original value was close to the modern one but derived from a theory that was already obsolete by 1925. The constant survived the theory that produced it, which is not a thing that happens often. As one review put it, "the letter α has outlived three revolutions in physics and remains undefeated."

The quantum electrodynamic era

With the arrival of Dirac's equation (1928) and the renormalisation programme (1947–1950), α became the single free parameter of quantum electrodynamics — the theory of light and electrons. Everything else in QED is a prediction. The anomalous magnetic moment of the electron, g/2 = 1.001 159 652 18, agrees with theory to more than ten significant figures; it is the most precisely verified prediction in all of science, and it is a prediction written entirely in powers of α.

Measurement

Because α is dimensionless, it can be extracted from any process whose rate depends on it. Over the past century the error bars have shrunk from a few parts in a thousand to eighty-one parts in a trillion.

The 2020 result, published in Nature, displaced the CODATA recommended value by about two and a half standard deviations — a polite way of saying that two very careful experiments disagreed, and the community is still deciding whom to believe. [3]

Running of the constant

Strictly speaking, α is not a constant. Because the vacuum is polarisable — virtual electron–positron pairs screen the bare charge — the effective coupling grows with the energy at which you probe it. At the mass of the Z boson, α−1 ≈ 127.95.

This is not a paradox and does not mean the constant changes in time; it means that "the charge of the electron" is a scale-dependent bookkeeping device. Still, it does spoil the neatness of the story: the integer 137 is not a property of nature, it is a property of nature measured at low energy by human beings on a rock. The numerology described below generally ignores this.

Stories and numerology

No accepted derivation of α exists. It is a free parameter of the Standard Model: you measure it, you put it in, and the theory works. This gap has attracted an unusual density of stories, and the stories are part of the constant's cultural life. They are collected here with appropriate scepticism.

Eddington's 137

Arthur Stanley Eddington, the astrophysicist who confirmed general relativity and then spent the rest of his life trying to unify physics by pure thought, became convinced that α−1 was exactly 137. His argument rested on the claim that the number of independent quantities needed to specify the state of the universe was connected to 137 via combinatorial reasoning about the symmetric group — a "proof" delivered with great confidence and no derivable prediction. [5]

When measurements drifted upwards — 137.036 and climbing — Eddington is said to have revised his result to 137 + 1/137 ≈ 137.0073, which was still wrong, and to have attributed the discrepancy to experimental error. His contemporaries were not gentle. The episode became the standard cautionary tale in physics: a beautiful number is not evidence of a beautiful theory.

Eddington's reputation never fully recovered, though his earlier work remains foundational. The word "Eddingtonian" is occasionally used, unkindly, to describe a numerological argument dressed in tensor notation.

Pauli and room 137

Wolfgang Pauli, one of the founders of quantum mechanics and a man famously sceptical of mysticism, died on 15 December 1958 in the Rotkreuz hospital in Zürich. The room number was 137. The coincidence was noted immediately, and Pauli — who had spent decades thinking about α — is reported to have remarked to the attending nurse that this was a sign he would not be leaving. [6]

The anecdote is repeated in nearly every popular account of the constant. Its details vary between tellings, the number of witnesses is unclear, and at least one biographer treats the reported remark as a story told afterwards rather than a documented utterance. It survives because it is too neat to check, which is precisely the mechanism by which all the stories in this section survive.

Pauli also had a well-known effect named after him — the tendency of experimental apparatus to fail in his presence — which is likewise unverifiable and likewise beloved. He would probably have enjoyed the room number. He would certainly have demanded a derivation.

Feynman's wall

Richard Feynman, in his 1985 popular lectures on QED, put the situation as plainly as anyone has:

"It's a crazy number. It has been a mystery ever since it was discovered more than fifty years ago, and all good theoretical physicists put this number up on their wall and worry about it. Immediately you would like to know where this number for a coupling comes from: is it related to pi, or perhaps to the base of natural logarithms? Nobody knows. It's one of the greatest damn mysteries of physics: a magic number that comes to us with no understanding by man." [1]

The phrase "on their wall" has been taken up as a piece of shared vocabulary among physicists; a number "on the wall" is one that is known, measured, used daily, and completely unexplained. There are not many left — most constants have either been derived or absorbed into definitions by the SI redefinition of 2019. α remains on the wall.

Dirac's large numbers

Paul Dirac noticed that the ratio of the electromagnetic to the gravitational force between an electron and a proton is about 1039, and that the age of the universe expressed in atomic units is also about 1039. He proposed that this coincidence was not a coincidence but a law, and that the gravitational constant must therefore vary with time.[7]

The hypothesis is now disfavoured by lunar laser ranging and by the fossil record of the Oklo natural reactor, both of which constrain any variation far below what Dirac needed. But the style of argument — a large dimensionless number, a coincidence, an inference — is the same style that produced every failed explanation of α, and Dirac's prestige made the style respectable for a generation.

The pyramid problem

Because 137 is close to an integer, and because the human eye finds integers where nature has none, α has attracted a persistent fringe literature. The most durable claim is that the Great Pyramid of Giza encodes 137 in the ratio of its height to its base, or in the number of courses of stone, or in the angle of a shaft — the details shift with the author.

The general difficulty with such claims is that any structure with a few dozen measurements yields thousands of ratios, and 137 is not a hard number to reach by combination and rounding. The same arithmetic trick recovers the speed of light, the precession of Mercury, and (in one memorable pamphlet) the telephone number of the author's brother-in-law. The claims are listed here for completeness and should not be mistaken for physics.[8]

Other attempts and curiosities

  • 137 as a sum. Several authors have noted that 137 is prime, that it is the fourth centred 34-gonal number, and that it has a variety of other number-theoretic properties. None of these properties singles out 137, and none has led to a prediction.
  • 1/137 in base 2 and base 16. Enthusiasts have searched the binary and hexadecimal expansions of α for meaningful strings. This is the physics equivalent of finding a face in a cloud; with enough digits, every number contains every message.
  • The 137.036 problem. Every numerological scheme must explain not only 137 but the 0.036, and then the 0.000 000 084, and then the next digit. Historically this is where the schemes die: they are calibrated against a value that has since moved.
  • The "it must be an integer" instinct. There is no reason a dimensionless constant of nature should be an integer. The mass ratio of the muon to the electron is 206.768; nobody writes articles about 207.
  • Speculative derivations. Grand unified theories, the string-theory landscape and various anthropic arguments constrain α but do not predict it. In the landscape picture, α takes different values in different vacua and we observe 1/137 because complex chemistry — and therefore observers — requires a small coupling.

Possible variation over time

If α were not constant in time, the entire edifice of the previous section would become even more awkward. Two lines of evidence have been pursued.

Quasar absorption spectra. In 2011 a team led by John Webb reported a spatial dipole in the value of α inferred from absorption lines in quasar light, at the level of a few parts in a million over billions of years.[9] The result has been contested repeatedly; independent analyses of larger samples have generally not reproduced it, and systematic errors in the calibration of the spectrographs remain the leading explanation.

The Oklo reactor. A natural fission reactor operated in Gabon about 1.8 billion years ago. The isotopic ratios left behind depend on the neutron capture cross-sections of samarium and gadolinium, which depend on α. The measured ratios are consistent with no change at the level of a few parts in 1017 per year — one of the tightest constraints in all of physics, obtained from a hole in the ground.

Atomic clocks. Modern optical clocks comparing different atomic transitions constrain the present-day drift of α to less than 10−17 per year. So far, nothing.

In popular culture

The number 137 has a small but persistent life outside physics.

  • The phrase "the fine-structure constant" is a well-worn shorthand in science fiction for "the thing we do not understand"; it appears in this capacity in several novels and at least one film, generally spoken by a character looking at a whiteboard.
  • Feynman's "magic number" remark is among the most-quoted lines in popular science writing, usually with the 0.08542455 replaced by 137 and the caveat removed.
  • The Pauli room-number story circulates widely on physics-department corridors and appears in more biographies than its documentation supports.
  • A number of musicians and artists have titled works "137" after the constant. The connection is usually thematic rather than mathematical.

Notes

  1. The exact value of α is not known; only its measured value and an uncertainty are known. Statements of the form "α = 1/137" are approximations, sometimes deliberately so.
  2. The SI redefinition of 2019 fixed the numerical values of e, ħ and c, which means α is now the only one of the four quantities in its definition that is measured rather than defined. Its uncertainty is therefore the uncertainty of the measurement, full stop.
