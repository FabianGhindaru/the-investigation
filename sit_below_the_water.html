Caliburn, Excalibur, and the Trouble With Two Swords

Posted by Eibhín Ó Cearúill · Sunday, 6 March 2011 · 17 comments

People keep telling me that Excalibur and the sword in the stone are the same sword. I've been told this at a wedding, in a lift in Dublin, and once by a man in Tuam who wouldn't let it go and eventually bought me a pint to make his point, which was a lot. I get why people think it. I thought it myself for years. It isn't true though.

There are at least two swords, maybe three if you count the ones that get a name and then disappear for the rest of the story. And the confusion isn't medieval, which is what everyone assumes. It gets built up slowly between about 1136 and the middle of the nineteenth century, mostly by people not paying attention, and then the Victorians put it in stone and the films copied the Victorians.

ICaliburnus

The oldest version we can actually use is Geoffrey of Monmouth, the Historia Regum Britanniae, around 1136. Geoffrey doesn't have Excalibur. He has Caliburnus, the sword Arthur is girded with, forged in Avalon, and he uses it at the end to kill Mordred's men. Fine sword. No stone. No anvil. Nobody pulling anything out of anything. If you go to Geoffrey looking for the sword in the stone you'll come away annoyed, which is how I felt the first time I read it properly, having been promised a stone.

Geoffrey writes in Latin and latinises a Welsh name. The Welsh is Caledfwlch. Caled hard, bwlch cleft or notch, so something like "hard-notch," which is a decent name for a sword and a strange thing to build a whole argument on. It's almost certainly related to the Irish Caladbolg, Fergus mac Róich's sword, the one that cut the tops off three hills. Whether the Welsh borrowed from the Irish or both inherited it from something older I can't tell you. People with better Welsh and better Irish than mine have been at it a hundred years without settling. I'd lean shared inheritance. I wouldn't fight anyone over it.

IIThe Stone, Which Is Newer Than You Think

The sword in the stone comes from Robert de Boron, the Merlin, around 1200. He invented it. Sword in an anvil on a stone, anvil held there by a spell, only the true king gets it out. It's a good image and what it's actually about is legitimacy — the difference between a man who takes a crown and a man who's chosen to hold one. It was never a magic weapon. Nobody in 1200 was excited about the metallurgy.

Then the Vulgate Cycle comes along, 1215 to about 1235, and the Post-Vulgate after it, and they do what cycles do, which is absorb everything until the joints stop showing. The stone sword and the lake sword end up in the same house. Nobody ever properly decides which is which. These were compiled by people with a deadline and a patron and no filing system worth the name.

IIIMalory Knew, And Malory Blinked

By 1485 Malory has the whole mess and — this is the bit that gets skipped — he knows perfectly well there are two swords. In the Morte Darthur Arthur pulls the sword from the stone, then later it breaks in the fight with King Pellinore (I get his name wrong every single time, it's Pellinore not Pellinor), and Merlin takes him to the lake and he's given Excalibur to replace it. First sword broken, second sword a replacement, not the same object, and Malory says so in plain English and is not being subtle.

What Malory also does is call the second one Excalibur in a text where the first one never got a name at all, and the reader's brain does the cheap thing, which is to assume one hero one sword. Tennyson picks up the cheap reading, hands it to the Pre-Raphaelites, they hand it to everybody, and by 1950 it's in films. "Excalibur" itself is a French mangling. Escalibor, Excalibor, further mangling of Caliburnus, which is a latinisation of a Welsh compound. Four languages, something lost at every crossing. That's why it now sounds like a brand of disinfectant.

Small aside

There's a place in Wexford called Camlann Cross. Not "Camlann View" on a housing estate, not "near Camlann" — Camlann. A crossroads, a bog, and a bit of a stone with a cross cut into it that the OPW dates to about the twelfth century, which is six hundred years too late for any of this. Locals call it Arthur's Grave. There's nothing in it. I've been there twice. The second time a man in a Fiat asked me if I was lost.

I'm not saying the last battle was fought in a bog in Wexford. I'm just saying the name is on the Ordnance Survey since the 1840s and nobody's ever explained it, and five hundred years of antiquarians have hunted Camlann through Somerset and Cornwall without once thinking to knock on a door in Wexford. I'm not saying. I'm just saying.

IVWhere That Leaves Us

Caliburn is Geoffrey's Latin, Caledfwlch is the Welsh it came from, Excalibur is the French mangling of the Latin. The stone is Robert de Boron and belongs to a different argument about kingship entirely. The lake sword is a replacement for a broken sword and Malory tells you so on the page in about four sentences.

If you want one thing to carry out of this — and it's a long post, I accept that — the sword that proves you're king and the sword that wins the battle were never the same sword, and Arthur needed both. I've been trying to work out why that bothers me and I think it's because it means the proof and the work were separate problems from the start. Which is a whole other post. Possibly a whole other blog.

Three Notes on Mordred

Posted by Eibhín Ó Cearúill · Saturday, 19 February 2011 · 9 comments

Mordred gets a bad deal. He isn't a villain in the earliest material, he's a casualty of it. The incest is a twelfth-century addition doing a specific political job at a specific moment, and the May Day massacre is the only thing he does in the whole cycle that's genuinely his own idea, and nobody ever wants to talk about it because it's unpleasant. Which is, I'd argue, why it's there.
