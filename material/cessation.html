This has grown teeth in town. My department keeps getting asked about it, so I've transcribed the parts of Tuesday's Chronicle piece that matter. Elisions are mine. Archivist, 28, Found Dead in Bed Coroner rules suicide after autopsy finds no wound, no poison, and no cause MANCHESTER, Oct. 7. Julian Mercer, a twenty-eight-year-old archivist employed by the county records office, was found dead in his bed at his apartment on Harrow Street shortly after nine o'clock Monday morning. [...] no defensive wounds, no intoxicants in his blood, and no organic trauma. "An impossible, voluntary cessation of autonomic brainstem function." Medical examiner's report, Oct. 8, 1991 The medical examiner's office gave that phrase [...] as the cause of death. Asked to elaborate, a spokesperson said only that the report "stands as written." No note was found. The bed was made. His shoes had been placed together on the floor beside it, toes to the wall. [...] "We don't have a box for this," the coroner said. I knew him by sight only. Sad business, but I've read a hundred suicides in the county paper and none of them looked like this one. What I cannot get past is the sentence. Examiners do not write like that in a public document. Papers do not print it unless the source has run out of things to say. Any pathologists on the group? Does that phrase mean anything, or is it a long way of writing "unknown"? -- Dana Parsons | Bailey/Howe Library | d_parsons@uvm.edu "Manuscripts don't burn."

> "An impossible, voluntary cessation of autonomic brainstem function." It means nothing. There is no such diagnosis. Every death is a cessation of autonomic function. "Voluntary" asserts something no autopsy can show, and "impossible" is a man who has run out of forms. Toxicology takes two to four weeks at the state lab. Whatever they ruled on Tuesday, it was not based on the blood. Vermont also has no coroners. It's a medical examiner system, and if the Chronicle can't get that right in the first four paragraphs I would not trust it on the shoes. The dull explanation is that men of twenty-eight die in bed from undiagnosed conduction defects. Long QT, Brugada, a few per hundred thousand a year, first symptom last symptom. A clean autopsy doesn't exclude them, and you only find out afterwards by putting the family on an ECG. The arms and the shoes are not testimony. They are what a shaken colleague told a reporter who needed a lede. What is actually worth reading here is the folklore. Walter Cannon, "Voodoo Death," American Anthropologist 44:169-181, 1942. He collected reports of people dying under a curse and blamed sustained sympathetic overactivity. It is a serious paper by a serious physiologist and it is almost certainly wrong. Every case is secondhand. Not one has a necropsy. All of them take place in a society that expects the death, and Cannon was careful to point that out. The people quoting him are not. Mercer was not under a curse. Occam's razor. -- Peter Sutton || Dept. of Physiology || Hanover, NH
